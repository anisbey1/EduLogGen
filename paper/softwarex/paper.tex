\title{EduLogGen: Analysing, generating, validating, and benchmarking synthetic educational interaction logs}

\author[a]{Anis Bey\corref{cor1}}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author. E-mail: \href{mailto:beyanisse@gmail.com}{beyanisse@gmail.com}. ORCID: \url{https://orcid.org/0000-0001-9410-0851}}
\address[a]{Higher School of Management Sciences, Annaba, Algeria}

\begin{abstract}
Interaction logs from learning platforms are central to learning analytics
but are personal data that are hard to share, so methods are often evaluated
on a single private dataset and rarely against a known ground truth.
EduLogGen is an open-source Python package that ingests real logs with
pseudonymisation, analyses them, fits interpretable Markov and semi-Markov
generators, generates seeded synthetic logs, validates them against the real
data with fidelity metrics and privacy indicators, and benchmarks generators
under a versioned protocol on held-out learners. An experimental layer
generates data with ground truth: labelled anomalies, controlled behavioural
manipulations with a manipulation check, and behavioural profiles. On seven
modules of the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), Markov
generators reduced the bigram error of an independent baseline by about two
thirds, only the semi-Markov generator reproduced the timing between active
days, in six of seven modules profiles learned without outcome data separated
learners with 0--11\% pass rates from those with 72--93\%, and the whole study ran in ten minutes on a laptop.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}

Interaction logs from learning management systems are the raw material of
learning analytics and educational data mining~\cite{romero2020edm}. They are
also personal data: sharing them across institutions, attaching them to
papers, or using them in teaching is restricted by privacy law and
institutional
policy~\cite{drachsler2016delicate}. As a result, many methods are evaluated
on a single private dataset, results are hard to reproduce, and
methods for tasks such as anomaly detection or learner clustering are rarely
tested against a known ground truth.

Synthetic data are a common answer to this tension~\cite{jordon2022synthetic},
but educational logs have properties that generic tabular generators do not
model: events are ordered within sessions, sessions are nested in learners and
courses, time spent before the next action depends on the activity, and
activity follows the course calendar. Researchers who want synthetic logs
therefore write one-off scripts whose fidelity and privacy are seldom
measured.

EduLogGen is a Python package that makes this workflow explicit and
reproducible. It (i) ingests real logs from common formats through a
declarative mapping with pseudonymisation; (ii) sessionises and analyses them;
(iii) fits interpretable sequence generators; (iv) generates seeded synthetic
corpora; (v) validates synthetic against real data with fidelity metrics and
privacy indicators; and (vi) benchmarks generators under versioned protocols.
An experimental layer generates data with \emph{known} ground truth --
labelled anomalies, deliberate behavioural manipulations with a manipulation
check, and behavioural profiles -- so that detection and clustering methods
can be scored against it.

\section{Software description}

\subsection{Architecture}

EduLogGen is a pure-Python, fully typed package (one required dependency,
PyYAML) organised in layers with enforced import rules (checked by the test
suite): a core with immutable data models (events, sessions, datasets,
models, annotations); input/output (CSV, TSV, JSON Lines, Parquet; atomic,
fingerprinted corpus and model directories); analysis; generators; validation;
benchmarking; scenarios (the experimental layer); evaluation; visualisation;
and a command-line interface. Third-party readers, generators, metrics and
anomaly types can be added through entry-point plugins.

Every random step derives its seed from one user seed, every artifact records
the fingerprints of its inputs, and models are stored as JSON rather than
pickles, so a result can be traced to its data, parameters and seed and
regenerated bit-for-bit.

\subsection{Functionalities}

\paragraph{Ingestion and sessionisation} A YAML mapping assigns source columns
to canonical fields (learner, timestamp, activity, event type, course, score,
success, duration), with optional salted hashing of identifiers, timezone
handling, and a value-free data quality report. Sessions come from explicit
identifiers, an idle timeout, or both.

\paragraph{Analysis} Event and activity mixes, $n$-grams, transition
matrices, session lengths and durations, dwell times per activity, navigation
graphs, per-activity profiles, timezone-aware temporal patterns (hour,
weekday, week, deadlines), and the same statistics stratified by course,
week, cohort or any metadata field.

\paragraph{Generators} \texttt{markov} (order-$k$ with back-off),
\texttt{semi\_markov} (Markov transitions with per-activity dwell-time
distributions~\cite{yu2010hsmm}), and an \texttt{independent} baseline. Models
also learn session lengths, sessions per learner, when sessions start (hour
and day), and which activities accompany each event type. Generated
identifiers are re-mapped so no source identifier is reproduced.

\paragraph{Validation} Real and synthetic corpora are compared on marginal
(event-type total variation distance, activity Jensen--Shannon divergence),
structural (session length Kolmogorov--Smirnov, duration Wasserstein),
temporal, sequential (bigram and transition divergence) and navigation
metrics, plus privacy indicators (exact session duplication, rare $n$-gram
replay, nearest-neighbour distances). A detailed mode ranks where synthetic
data differ most (tokens, transitions, hours, groups).

\paragraph{Benchmarking} A versioned protocol (\texttt{session\_fidelity\_v1})
splits learners into training and holdout sets, fits each generator on the
training learners only, generates several seeded samples the size of the
holdout, and reports every metric as mean and spread, so generators are
compared on unseen learners.

\paragraph{Experimental layer} (i) Anomaly injection: five types (event
frequency, abnormal timing, unexpected transition, repetition, inactivity),
each labelled as an invalid workflow or unusual-but-valid behaviour and
stored as separate annotations, with an evaluator for detector predictions
(ROC-AUC, average precision, precision/recall). (ii) Controls: re-weighting
event types, scaling dwell times, fixing session lengths or sessions per
learner, and course calendars with deadline surges, verified by an automatic
manipulation check against an uncontrolled baseline. (iii) Behavioural
profiles learned by seeded $k$-means++~\cite{arthur2007kmeanspp} on training
learners, imported from a mapping, or defined manually by their behaviour;
mixtures of profiles are generated with learner-level ground truth, and
\texttt{evaluate\_clustering} reports the adjusted Rand
index~\cite{hubert1985ari}, normalised mutual information and purity.

\subsection{Quality assurance}
The package has 1{,}008 tests with 99\% line and branch coverage, strict
static typing, and continuous integration on Python 3.11--3.13 that also runs
an end-to-end pipeline and builds the documentation.

\section{Illustrative example}

\subsection{Command-line workflow}
A typical session uses six commands; every output directory records the
fingerprints of its inputs and the seed:
\begin{verbatim}
eduloggen ingest --input events.csv --mapping mapping.yaml --output corpus/
eduloggen sessionize --input corpus/ --output sessions/
eduloggen fit --input sessions/ --generator semi_markov --output model/
eduloggen generate --model model/ --n-sessions 5000 --seed 7 --output synthetic/
eduloggen validate --real sessions/ --synthetic synthetic/ --output report/
eduloggen benchmark --input sessions/ --output benchmark/
\end{verbatim}
The same steps are available from Python, together with the experimental
layer:
\begin{verbatim}
import eduloggen as elg
profiles = elg.fit_profiles(train, n_profiles=4, seed=0)   # training only
groups = profiles.assign(holdout)                           # nearest profile
mix = elg.generate_profiles(profiles, 10_000, seed=1)       # + ground truth
report = elg.evaluate_clustering(mix.annotations, predictions)
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Case study: the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset}
We applied EduLogGen to the seven modules of the 2014J presentation of
OULAD~\cite{kuzilek2017oulad} (10{,}143 learners; Table~\ref{tab:oulad}).
OULAD records clicks per learner, resource and \emph{day}, without time of
day, and the order of rows within a day is not meaningful. We therefore
defined an event as an active learner-day (event type: the activity type with
most clicks that day) and a session as a learner's week of the module, so that
sequences and inter-event gaps (whole days) are observed rather than
invented; hour-of-day and weekday analyses were not used. The conversion and
analysis scripts are in the repository (\texttt{studies/oulad/}) and all
results below are reproduced by them with seed 0.

\paragraph{Fidelity} The \texttt{session\_fidelity\_v1} protocol fits each
generator on 70\% of the learners and compares three synthetic samples with
the remaining 30\%. As a reference it also compares the two real halves,
which shows how much two samples of real learners differ
(Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fidelity}). All generators matched
the event-type mix and session lengths within that real-versus-real range.
The independent baseline, which ignores order, had a bigram total variation
distance of 0.220; first- and second-order Markov and semi-Markov generators
reduced it to 0.073--0.079 (reference 0.038). Only the semi-Markov generator,
which learns time spent per activity, reproduced the distribution of gaps
between active days (Kolmogorov--Smirnov 0.013 against 0.281 for the
others; reference 0.010). The second-order model did not improve on the
first-order one for these short weekly sequences.

\paragraph{Reading privacy indicators} Between 66\% and 75\% of synthetic
sessions exactly matched a real session, but so did 77\% of real holdout
sessions with respect to the training learners: with at most seven active
days and a handful of activity types per week, identical weekly patterns are
common among real learners. Duplication is therefore not, on its own,
evidence of memorisation here; EduLogGen reports the real-versus-real
reference so that such indicators can be interpreted.

\paragraph{Profiles} Automatic profiles ($k=4$) were learned on training
learners only, and holdout learners were assigned to the nearest profile.
Without using outcomes, six of the seven modules produced a
low-engagement profile (2.6--6.9 active weeks on average) whose holdout
learners passed or obtained a distinction in 0--11\% of cases; in every
module the most successful profile reached 72--93\%. In the smallest module
(AAA, 357 learners) the least active profile still passed in 57\% of cases.
A profile dominated by forum activity appeared in all seven modules.
The normalised mutual information between profile and final result was
modest (0.10--0.30), and part of the association reflects persistence, since
learners who withdraw stop generating activity. These profiles are
descriptive behavioural clusters, not validated learner types. Generating a
synthetic population from the profiles and clustering it again recovered the
known profile of each synthetic learner with an adjusted Rand index of
0.78--0.92.

\paragraph{Detection with ground truth} We injected 5\% unexpected
transitions (invalid workflow) and 5\% repetitions (unusual but valid) into
real holdout sessions and scored a simple detector, the mean negative
log-likelihood of each session's transitions under a smoothed bigram model
fitted on training learners. It reached a ROC-AUC of 0.88--0.92 and an
average precision of 0.47--0.57, giving a known-truth baseline against which
better detectors can be compared.

\paragraph{Cost} The full study (seven modules, four generators, three seeds,
profiles and detection) took ten minutes on a laptop CPU with the pure-Python
implementation; the largest module (167{,}164 learner-days) took 136~s.

\section{Impact}
EduLogGen addresses three recurring obstacles in learning analytics
research. First, \emph{sharing}: a research group can publish synthetic logs
fitted on data it cannot release, together with a validation report showing
how close they are and what the privacy indicators say, so that others can
develop and test methods before applying for access to the real data.
Second, \emph{ground truth}: anomaly detectors and clustering methods can be
scored on data where the anomalous sessions and the generating profiles are
known, separately for invalid workflows and unusual-but-valid behaviour; the
OULAD case study gives reference values for a simple detector and for
profile recovery. Third, \emph{reproducibility}: seeds, fingerprints,
versioned protocols and JSON models make every number traceable and
regenerable, and the benchmark compares generators on unseen learners
against a real-versus-real reference rather than on the training data.

The package also supports teaching, by giving students realistic logs
without personal data, and controlled studies of methods, for example how a
detector degrades when deadline surges or new activity types are introduced
through the controls and calendars. Its plugin interfaces let users add
readers for other platforms, new generators (e.g.\ neural sequence models)
and metrics while reusing the validation and benchmarking machinery.

\section{Conclusions}
EduLogGen provides an end-to-end, reproducible workflow for synthetic
educational interaction logs, from ingestion to validated and benchmarked
synthetic corpora, and an experimental layer that generates data with known
ground truth. On OULAD, interpretable Markov and semi-Markov generators
reproduced event mixes, session structure and, for the semi-Markov model,
timing, within or near the variation between real learner samples, while
remaining fast. Planned work includes simulated learning outcomes with
leakage-safe prediction splits, scenario files with a dedicated command-line
interface, further anomaly types, and differentially private generators.
